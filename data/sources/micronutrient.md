# Source Library: Micronutrients, Vitamins & Nutrigenomics

_Compiled research on genotype-linked micronutrient needs (MTHFR, VDR, APOE4, HFE, TRPM6, etc.) and population-level vitamin/mineral-mortality studies (Vitamin D, B12, Selenium, Magnesium, Omega-3, K2, multivitamins)._

Section 4 of the system prompt prohibits exactly this kind of output. When drawing on this file, translate "if you carry variant X, take Y" into "this class of variant is associated with Z in the research population — worth discussing with a clinician who can test for it," never as a direct instruction to supplement. Vitamin D
1. Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline (2024)
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38950338/
If you are aged 75+, a meta-analysis of 25 RCTs (~49,879 people) links vitamin D supplementation (~900 IU/day) to lower all-cause mortality, so daily low-dose D is reasonable. If you are a healthy adult aged 19–74 eating normally, you generally should NOT routinely supplement or screen.
2. Ye et al., Nutrition Journal (Mar 2024, NHANES)
https://doi.org/10.1186/s12937-024-00914-8
If you have hypertension and your serum 25(OH)D is below ~50 nmol/L (especially <25), your all-cause mortality risk is elevated (HR up to 1.97), and supplement users had lower all-cause mortality — so supplement.
Note: Observational.
3. Ther Drug Monit (2024, NHANES 2007–2018, n=10,793)
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38967521/
Higher 25(OH)D was robustly associated with reduced all-cause mortality (but not CVD- or cancer-specific). The dose cut was ≥800 IU/day — so if your level is low, ≥800 IU/day is the supported maintenance target.
4. Zheng et al., Front. Nutr. (Apr 2025)
https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2025.1505616/full
In critically ill adults, 19 RCTs (2,754 patients) were pooled to assess vitamin D on short-term mortality — relevant only for acute/ICU contexts, not a general longevity recommendation.
Folate / MTHFR (genetic)
5. Chen et al., Front. Genet. (Nov 2025)
https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/genetics/articles/10.3389/fgene.2025.1693729/full
If you carry the MTHFR C677T TT or CT genotype, expert consensus recommends 0.8–1 mg/day folate (ideally L-methylfolate), especially for women of childbearing age starting ≥3 months pre-conception. Wild-type CC needs only dietary folate.
Note: Hard evidence is for birth-defect prevention, not lifespan.
Omega-3 / APOE4 (genetic)
6. reporting Shinto et al., Neurology (Aug 2024, n=102, age 75+)
https://www.parsemus.org/2024/12/omega-3-fatty-acids-may-protect-brain-health/
An EPA-dominant omega-3 may benefit APOE4 carriers without dementia, while a DHA-dominant one may benefit non-carriers with mild-to-moderate Alzheimer’s — so genotype should flip the EPA:DHA ratio you recommend.
Note: Results in carriers are genuinely inconsistent across trials.
7. Nutrients (Sep 2025, overview of SRs, ~26,881 people, age 40+)
https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/17/18/3002
If you are 40+ with mild cognitive impairment or low omega-3 status, EPA+DHA shows a modest cognitive benefit that scales with higher dose and longer duration. If your status is already adequate, expected benefit is small — supplement only if intake is low.
8. Mattumpuram et al., Clin. Transl. Discovery (Nov 2025, 42 studies, 176,253 people)
https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ctd2.70094
If you have established cardiovascular disease or high CV risk, omega-3 supplementation was associated with significant reductions in CV mortality, CHD, MI, and fatal MI — so supplement in secondary-prevention users specifically.
9. Kim et al., J. Clin. Med. (Dec 2024)
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11722046/
A meta-analysis examined omega-3 (EPA, DPA, DHA) as a blood biomarker to screen groups at high risk of cardiovascular mortality and sudden cardiac death — useful as a rationale for using omega-3 blood index as a risk INPUT, not just a supplement output.
10. Dinu et al., Eur. J. Prev. Cardiol. (Oct 2024)
https://academic.oup.com/eurjpc/article/31/15/1863/7692830
Benefits of omega-3 on coronary revascularization and the specific role of EPA remain debated/controversial — include as the counterweight showing the cardiovascular case is not settled.
11. Scientific Reports (Oct 2025, NHANES)
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-21193-1
Among people with existing cardiovascular disease, dietary omega-3 intake was examined against all-cause and CVD mortality — supports a “if you already have CVD, raise omega-3 intake” recommendation.
Note: Observational.
Magnesium
12. Ma, Li & Liu, Front. Nutr. (Oct 2025, NHANES + hospital cohort)
https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2025.1674062/full
If you are a cancer survivor with a high Magnesium Depletion Score (≥3), your all-cause, cancer, and CVD mortality risk is significantly higher after adjustment — so flag low magnesium status for correction in this group.
13. Lu et al., Biol. Trace Elem. Res. (2025, NHANES)
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12011-024-04395-y
In people with gout or hyperuricemia, dietary magnesium intake was evaluated against all-cause and cause-specific mortality across ~58,000+ person-years — supports raising dietary Mg in this subgroup.
14. Front. Nutr. (Oct 2024)
https://doaj.org/article/50a0af4f9a834ea592f2eff5f5ff4786
In people with atherosclerotic CVD, higher dietary magnesium was linearly associated with lower all-cause mortality (highest vs lowest quartile HR 0.63), and BMI modified the effect — so factor BMI alongside Mg intake.
15. Atherosclerosis (2025, PLCO cohort)
https://www.atherosclerosis-journal.com/article/S0021-9150(25)01426-1/abstract
A magnesium-rich diet was associated with lower cardiovascular mortality, mediated partly by oxidative-balance score — supports diet-first Mg messaging over pills.
16. Liu et al., J. Health Popul. Nutr. (Aug 2024, NHANES)
https://doaj.org/article/c619109c820f46c5b4c369177e8f5572
In rheumatoid arthritis patients, dietary magnesium intake was assessed against all-cause mortality as an anti-inflammatory nutrient — supports Mg adequacy in chronic-inflammatory subgroups.
Vitamin B12 (strongly age-dependent)
17. Marino et al., Alzheimer’s & Dementia (2025, Framingham, n=1,994, mean age 60)
https://alz-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/alz.70864
Higher vitamin B12 from mid- to late life was related to slower rates of cognitive decline — supports maintaining higher-end B12 from midlife onward.
18. reporting UCSF, Annals of Neurology (2025, DOI 10.1002/ana.27200)
https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/02/250218203749.htm
Older healthy adults with lower-but-still-“normal” B12 showed more white-matter damage and slower processing speed — implying current “normal” thresholds may be too low for older adults; a strong age-based recommendation input.
19. Comprehensive review (Apr 2025)
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S266645932500040X
10–15% of people over 60 have B12 deficiency, largely from age-related gastric atrophy reducing absorption — so age >60 itself is a trigger to check/supplement B12.
20. Chua et al. (2025)
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12789806/
If you are 65+ with diabetes on metformin, you are at high risk of B12 deficiency from reduced gut absorption and should be screened — a clean gene-free “if on metformin + older, supplement B12” rule.
21. Shanghai Aging survey (2025, n=8,806, age ≥65)
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12757219/
B-complex vitamin intake and sleep quality showed an additive interaction with cognitive impairment in older adults — supports combining B-vitamin adequacy with sleep in your model.
22. Rosenberg (2024)
https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/03795721241228060
B12 deficiency in elders contributes to cognitive decline via cerebrovascular and direct neuronal damage, and treating it addresses a potentially reversible factor — mechanistic support for B12 correction in older adults.
Selenium
23. Cui et al., Redox Biology (Jul 2025)
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12302194
A systematic review/meta-analysis assessed circulating selenium status against all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality in population cohorts — the best recent umbrella source; relevant especially where soil/dietary selenium is low (much of Europe).
24. Schöttker et al., Eur. J. Epidemiol. (Jan 2024, ESTHER, n=7,186, age 50–74)
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10654-023-01091-4
If your serum SELENOP is below ~4.1 mg/L, your all-cause mortality risk is significantly higher (L-shaped relationship) — so low selenium status is a correction target.
25. Berlin Aging Study II, Clinical Epigenetics (Apr 2025)
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13148-025-01863-7
Low selenium, SELENOP, and GPx3 were associated with accelerated biological (DNA-methylation) aging — directly relevant to a longevity/biological-age engine.
26. Front. Neurol. (Jul 2024, NHANES)
https://doaj.org/article/c1580ba678eb4fe0bba89a78c8d7bd48
Blood selenium ≥192.96 µg/L was associated with lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.70), with the worst outcomes in those with low selenium plus stroke history (HR 2.31) — supports selenium adequacy especially in stroke survivors.
Multivitamins (cautionary — a “shouldn’t bother” finding)
27. Loftfield et al., JAMA Network Open (Jun 2024, NIH/NCI, n=390,124, up to 27 yrs)
https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2820369
In healthy adults, daily multivitamin use was NOT associated with lower all-cause, cancer, heart-disease, or cerebrovascular mortality — so your product should NOT recommend a blanket daily multivitamin to healthy users; target specific deficiencies instead.
Vitamin K2
28. Nutrients (Feb 2025, RCT post hoc, n=165)
https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/17/5/815
If you are a peri-/post-menopausal woman with low vitamin K status, low K status raises vascular calcification/stiffness risk, and 180 µg/day MK-7 for one year was tested on vascular parameters — supports MK-7 specifically in low-K, higher-CV-risk women.
29. Vossen et al. (linked MK-7 / coronary calcification trials, incl. JAMA Cardiology 2026)
https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacadv.2023.100643
Coronary artery calcification extent and progression strongly predict MI and mortality, and MK-7 supplementation has been trialed to slow it — use the 2026 JAMA Cardiology result as your primary K2 endpoint once you pull it.
